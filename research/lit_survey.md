## Summary

I surveyed six areas across arXiv (abstract-level verification via the arXiv API), ACL Anthology, GitHub license API, OpenAlex/DBLP for pre-arXiv classics, and vendor engineering docs. Headline honest findings: (1) **every** prompt-compression system in the literature is model-dependent (needs a small LM, an encoder, or fine-tuned soft tokens) — none is a tokenizer-agnostic, no-model converter, so TDF occupies a genuinely different point in the design space and is *not* directly beaten, but it also cannot claim their compression ratios (2x–480x vs your 1.1x–1.75x). (2) Your **greedy n-gram dictionary is a weaker instance of Re-Pair**, which is 1999, linear-time, and has published approximation bounds; the principled objective you're missing is **MDL / Krimp-style "does this pattern pay for its own code-table entry"** — which is exactly the criterion you hand-derived (`c*t − c*2 − t − 2`). (3) Your **sentence-density discriminator for nav-vs-prose is a rediscovery of text-density/link-density boilerplate detection** (Kohlschütter et al., WSDM 2010; jusText; trafilatura) — this is the place where your heuristic is most clearly already published. (4) **"Addressable elision" is partially novel**: the *mechanism* (declared omission + retrievable id + progressive disclosure) is now well-established at the agent/tool layer (Anthropic "just-in-time" context, MemGPT, MemWalker, PageIndex, RLMs, and a 2026 controlled study of progressive disclosure), but I found **no published work that puts declared, sized, gisted omission markers inside the serialized document format itself** as a converter output. That framing is defensible as novel; "the model can request the missing region" is not.

## 2. Grammar-based / dictionary compression

- **Sequitur** — Nevill-Manning & Witten, *JAIR* 7:67–82, 1997, [doi:10.1613/jair.374](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.374) (also [arXiv:cs/9709102](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9709102)). Linear-time, **online**, maintains two invariants (digram uniqueness, rule utility). Verified via DBLP `journals/jair/Nevill-ManningW97`.
- **Re-Pair** — Larsson & Moffat, "Offline Dictionary-Based Compression", *DCC 1999*, pp. 296–305, [doi:10.1109/DCC.1999.755679](https://doi.org/10.1109/DCC.1999.755679). Verified via DBLP `conf/dcc/LarssonM99`. Repeatedly replaces the **most frequent bigram**; linear time but memory-hungry.
- **Smallest Grammar Problem** — Charikar et al., *IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory*, 2005, [doi:10.1109/tit.2005.850116](https://doi.org/10.1109/tit.2005.850116) (verified via OpenAlex W2113004376). NP-hard; APX-hard (re-confirmed in [arXiv:2604.16302](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16302), 2026).
- **Approximation bounds** — Hucke, Jeż & Lohrey, [arXiv:1703.06061](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06061) and Bannai et al., [arXiv:1908.06428](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.06428): Re-Pair's approximation ratio lower bound improved to **Ω(log n / log log n)**; LZ78 is **Θ((n/log n)^{2/3})**, BISECTION **Θ(√(n/log n))**. Translation: **Re-Pair is provably not optimal, but is provably far better than LZ78-class schemes** and is the best practical greedy grammar compressor.
- **Re-Pair optimality case** — [arXiv:2202.08447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08447): Re-Pair is exactly optimal on Fibonacci words (first non-trivial family).
- **Scalability** — "Re-Pair In Small Space", [arXiv:1908.04933](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04933): the practical objection to Re-Pair is the **frequency table memory**, addressed with O(n log max(n,τ)) bits variants (incl. parallel/external-memory). For document-sized inputs (≤1M tokens) this is a non-issue.
- **Beyond SLPs** — Siyari & Gallé, "The Generalized Smallest Grammar Problem", [arXiv:1608.08927](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.08927): minimizing *non-recursive* grammars finds **smaller models than best SGP approximations on standard benchmarks, and rules that better capture natural-language syntactic structure**. Directly relevant: this is the version of grammar induction that produces human-meaningful phrases, which matters because your `§n` entries must remain readable.
- **MDL as the principled objective** — Vreeken, van Leeuwen & Siebes, **"Krimp: mining itemsets that compress"**, *DMKD* 2011, [doi:10.1007/s10618-010-0202-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-010-0202-x) (open access). The MDL formulation: *the best set of patterns is the set that compresses the database best*, selecting a **code table** where each candidate must pay for its own encoding. Sequence analogue: Tatti & Vreeken, **"The Long and the Short of It: Summarising Event Sequences with Serial Episodes"** (KDD 2012; arXiv mirror [arXiv:1902.02834](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.02834), verified via OpenAlex W2153982189) — MDL-based selection of *sequential* patterns, which is precisely your n-gram dictionary problem.

**Would Re-Pair beat your greedy seed-and-extend n-gram dictionary? Honest answer: probably yes on compression, at a cost you may not want to pay.**
- Your rule from `README.md:175-177` (`c*t − c*2 − t − 2 > 0`) **is** a per-entry MDL criterion, computed in *tokens* rather than bits. That's the right instinct and is already a "principled objective" — you should say so, citing Krimp/MDL, rather than calling it hand-tuned.
- Your README `:459` already documents the exact failure mode Re-Pair fixes: *"Overlapping dictionary entries never fired. 'A B C D' and 'B C D E'…"*. Greedy top-down maximal-phrase selection has no way to arbitrate overlaps; Re-Pair's bottom-up bigram recursion resolves this structurally and builds long phrases by composition instead of by extension.
- **Costs/tradeoffs to weigh:** (i) Re-Pair produces a *hierarchy* of rules (rules referencing rules) — cheap for a decompressor, but an LLM reading `§7 = §3 §5` cannot resolve it without expansion, so you'd need to flatten, which partially undoes the gain. (ii) Re-Pair is character/symbol-agnostic and will happily create non-word-boundary rules that destroy your plain-ASCII readability property. (iii) Your objective isn't "smallest grammar" — it's *smallest token count under an unknown BPE*, which is a **different and non-additive cost function** (BPE merges across boundaries; leading-space merging is exactly your key finding at `README.md:103-106`). Re-Pair's optimality results say nothing about that objective.
- **Concrete recommendation:** implement Re-Pair over a **word-level alphabet with a token-cost-weighted priority** (replace "most frequent bigram" with "bigram with max estimated token saving"), and gate every rule by the MDL/token-payback test you already have. That is a defensible, publishable variant: *token-cost-weighted Re-Pair with an MDL admission rule*. Benchmark it head-to-head against your greedy version on your 11 documents — that ablation is exactly the kind of evidence reviewers/readers want.

## 3. Near-duplicate and redundancy detection

- **Shingling / MinHash** — Broder, "On the resemblance and containment of documents", SEQUENCES 1997, [doi:10.1109/SEQUEN.1997.666900](https://doi.org/10.1109/SEQUEN.1997.666900) (verified OpenAlex W2132069633). Estimates **Jaccard** over k-shingles.
- **SimHash** — Charikar, "Similarity estimation techniques from rounding algorithms", STOC 2002, [doi:10.1145/509907.509965](https://doi.org/10.1145/509907.509965); made practical at web scale by **Manku, Jain & Das Sarma, "Detecting near-duplicates for web crawling", WWW 2007**, [doi:10.1145/1242572.1242592](https://doi.org/10.1145/1242572.1242592) (verified DBLP `conf/www/MankuJS07`) — 64-bit fingerprints, Hamming distance ≤3, 8B pages.
- **Suffix arrays for exact repeated substrings at scale** — Lee et al., "Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better", ACL 2022, [arXiv:2107.06499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06499); code `google-research/deduplicate-text-datasets`. They use **two** tools deliberately: (a) **ExactSubstr** via suffix array — finds all substrings repeated ≥50 tokens, exactly; (b) **NearDup** via MinHash+LSH for approximate document-level dupes. Notable data point: a single 61-word sentence repeated **60,000+ times** in C4.
- **Current standard practice** — FineWeb, [arXiv:2406.17557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557), ablates dedup strategies at 15T-token scale; **MinHash+LSH is the de-facto default** for near-duplicate documents in LLM data pipelines (datatrove/text-dedup implement it).

**Which is standard for your "Copyright 2023 Acme" vs "Copyright 2024 Acme" case?** Neither MinHash nor SimHash is ideal at that granularity — both are *document/paragraph-level* similarity estimators, and your strings are ~4 tokens long where MinHash signatures are statistically meaningless. The right tools for line-level boilerplate:
1. **Suffix automaton / suffix array over the concatenated document** to find all maximal repeated substrings and their occurrence counts in O(n) — this is what the ExactSubstr tool does, and it subsumes your `!R` boilerplate detection *and* your n-gram dictionary seeding into **one data structure**. Strong recommendation: this replaces two hand-tuned heuristics with one principled index.
2. For the *near*-duplicate variant (year changes), the classic solution is **pattern/template induction over aligned repeats**: find the maximal repeat, then treat the differing span as a slot. That is literally Re-Pair-with-gaps, and it connects back to §2 — you could emit `!R Copyright {} Acme` with per-occurrence slot fills. Your README `:363-364` says template induction died on the Kubernetes nav data (only 35/1015 items shared a 3-word prefix), but that was *list items*, not *page furniture*; the SEC filing and handbook are where it would pay.
3. SimHash remains the right tool if you ever dedupe **across documents** in a corpus-level TDF mode.

## 4. Structure-aware document representation

- **OTSL** — Lysak et al., ICDAR 2023, [arXiv:2305.03393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03393). 5-token vocabulary vs HTML's 28+, **~half the sequence length**, halved inference time, **always syntactically correct** output eliminating post-processing. Your README already cites it correctly. The transferable idea you may not have taken: OTSL's guarantee is *syntactic validity by construction* — your `!T n` declared row count (`README.md:168-169`) is a weaker version (detectable truncation, not impossible truncation). A grammar where malformed tables are unrepresentable would be strictly stronger.
- **DocTags / Docling** — [arXiv:2408.09869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09869) (Docling, **MIT**), [arXiv:2503.11576](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11576) (SmolDocling, 256M VLM emitting DocTags with location). Your "DocTags trap" analysis (`README.md:56-65`) is correct and well-supported: DocTags reduces *sequence length for SmolDocling* because tags are special vocabulary items; nothing in either paper claims tokenizer-agnostic efficiency.
- **hOCR** — spec 1.2, https://kba.github.io/hocr-spec/1.2/. Explicitly an **HTML subset** (`class="ocr_page"`, properties packed into `title` attributes, e.g. `bbox`). Verified from the spec text. For your purposes it is a **negative** example: it is maximally verbose (attributes-in-strings inside HTML) and is the canonical demonstration that reusing HTML for layout costs tokens. Good citation for "why we didn't do that".
- **Table format effects on LLM accuracy** — Sui et al., **"Table Meets LLM"**, WSDM 2024, [arXiv:2305.13062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13062). Directly relevant: seven structural-understanding tasks (cell lookup, row retrieval, size detection) and the finding that **performance varies with input table format, content order, role prompting, and partition marks**. This is the benchmark you should run TDF tables against — it isolates *format* as the variable, which is exactly your thesis and complements TOON.
- **Hierarchical / lazy-expansion representations**:
  - **RAPTOR** — ICLR 2024, [arXiv:2401.18059](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18059). Recursive embed→cluster→summarize into a tree; retrieve at multiple abstraction levels; **+20% absolute on QuALITY** with GPT-4.
  - **MemWalker** — [arXiv:2310.05029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05029). Tree of summary nodes; the LLM **navigates interactively**, and the authors emphasize **explainability: it pinpoints the relevant text segments**. This is the closest published cousin to skeleton+expand.
  - **PageIndex** — https://github.com/VectifyAI/PageIndex, MIT. "Vectorless, reasoning-based RAG": builds a **table-of-contents tree index**, LLM does tree search; claims **98.7% on FinanceBench**. Your README's characterization ("index only, doesn't compress the body") is accurate.
  - **GraphRAG** — [arXiv:2404.16130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130) — pre-generated community summaries; relevant as the "summarize-everything-eagerly" opposite pole to lazy expansion.

**Applicability:** OTSL and Table-Meets-LLM are directly usable (benchmark + design principle). RAPTOR/PageIndex/GraphRAG all require LLM calls to *build* the structure — incompatible with your no-model constraint — but your skeleton mode is the model-free analogue and you should say exactly that: *"a deterministic, zero-inference approximation of a RAPTOR/PageIndex tree, built from heading structure rather than clustering+summarization."*

## 5. Evaluation methodology

The defensible replacement for your ad-hoc A/B QA eval, in the order the compression literature actually uses:

1. **LongBench** ([arXiv:2308.14508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14508), ACL 2024) — 21 datasets, 6 categories, bilingual, unified format for automatic eval. **This is the single most-used benchmark in prompt compression** (LLMLingua-2, DAC, TPC, EHPC, the empirical study all report it). If you report one thing, report this.
2. **ZeroSCROLLS** ([arXiv:2305.14196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14196)) — zero-shot long-text NLU, 10 datasets, includes **information-fusing/aggregation** tasks; used alongside LongBench by LLMLingua-2 and TPC.
3. **GSM8K + BBH** — the standard **reasoning-retention** probes in this literature (LLMLingua, LLMLingua-2, DAC). Important for you: these test whether compression destroys chain-of-thought-relevant detail, and **number normalization is exactly the kind of transform that could break GSM8K** — this is the highest-value cheap experiment you can run.
4. **NaturalQuestions (multi-doc)** — LongLLMLingua's headline benchmark; also the standard position-bias/"lost in the middle" setup.
5. **RULER** ([arXiv:2404.06654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654), COLM 2024) — the *correct* replacement for vanilla needle-in-a-haystack: multi-needle, multi-hop tracing, aggregation. Key caveat you should quote: models score near-perfect on vanilla NIAH yet **collapse as length grows** — so NIAH alone is not evidence.
6. **HELMET** ([arXiv:2410.02694](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02694), ICLR 2025) — the strongest methodological critique available: **"synthetic tasks like NIAH do not reliably predict downstream performance"**, existing benchmarks give noisy signals, and the authors recommend **RAG tasks for fast iteration**. Cite this to justify whatever subset you choose.
7. **LongBench v2** ([arXiv:2412.15204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15204)) — 503 hard MCQs, 8k–2M words, includes a **"long structured data understanding"** category — the most on-target category that exists for a document converter. Human experts get 53.7%; best direct-answer model 50.1%.
8. **Table Meets LLM** ([arXiv:2305.13062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13062)) — for the table-encoding claims specifically.

**Metric design (from the literature, adapted to your lossless-by-default framing):** report the pair **(compression ratio, task accuracy retention %)** as a *curve*, not a point — this is universal in the field. Your unique advantage is that for the lossless tier you can additionally report **exact round-trip reconstruction**, which no prompt-compression paper can. Make that the headline: *"accuracy retention is 100% by construction for tiers 1–4; here is the measured curve for tier 5 (elision)."* Also worth reproducing: the empirical study's finding that **moderate compression sometimes *improves* LongBench scores** ([arXiv:2505.00019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00019)) — if TDF shows that, it's a strong result; if it doesn't, you need to know.

**One methodological warning:** 500xCompressor explicitly calls out **"potential data leakage during evaluation"** ([arXiv:2408.03094](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03094)) as a flaw in prior work — they pretrain on Arxiv Corpus and evaluate on *strictly unseen* sets. Your `attention.pdf` (arXiv 1706.03762) benchmark document is in every model's training data; any QA eval on it is contaminated. Swap in documents post-dating the model cutoff.

## 6. Is "addressable elision" novel? — honest assessment

**Prior art that clearly exists (the mechanism is not new):**

- **MemGPT** ([arXiv:2310.08560](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560), 2023) — OS-style **virtual context management**: tiered memory, paging between "fast" in-context and "slow" external storage, with the model issuing function calls to page data in. This is the canonical "declared omission with a retrieval handle", at the conversation/memory level.
- **MemWalker** ([arXiv:2310.05029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05029)) — tree of summary nodes the model navigates; the model sees gists and requests children.
- **Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"** (https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) — this is the closest *named* prior art to your framing and uses your exact vocabulary: *"just in time" context strategies … agents maintain **lightweight identifiers** (file paths, stored queries, web links) and use these references to dynamically load data into context at runtime"*, and *"enables **progressive disclosure**"*. It also explicitly notes that **reference metadata itself carries signal** (a filename in `tests/` vs `src/core_logic/`) — which is precisely the role your `!E x1 index 3856 2850 <gist>` fields play.
- **"Is Progressive Disclosure All You Need for Long-Context Agents?"** ([arXiv:2607.17598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17598), 2026) — **the most directly threatening paper to your novelty claim, and also the most useful.** First controlled study of the pattern on InfiniteBench across 3 harnesses × 3 model families. Findings you must engage with: gains **depend on the harness** and are **near zero when a strong agent already navigates the raw document well**; benefits become **decisive only when the corpus is too large to read**; and **a second level of routing never helps and sometimes breaks accuracy — one level is enough**. Their summary line — *"progressive disclosure buys context, not intelligence"* — is a direct challenge to your Kubernetes result, and their one-level finding validates your flat `!E` design over a nested one.
- **Recursive Language Models** (Zhang & Khattab, 2025; blog https://alexzhang13.github.io/blog/2025/rlm/, paper https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.24601, code https://github.com/alexzhang13/rlm) — context stored as a **variable in a REPL**; the root LM greps/partitions/sub-queries it and never sees the whole thing. GPT-5-mini RLM beats GPT-5 on OOLONG at lower cost, **no degradation at 10M+ tokens**. Same philosophy, different layer.
- **PageIndex** (MIT) and **RAPTOR** — gist-first, expand-on-demand trees.
- **RECOMP**'s empty-string / selective augmentation ([arXiv:2310.04408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04408)) is a degenerate declared omission (it declares "nothing useful here"), but with no retrieval handle.

**What I could not find, after targeted searching (this is where your novelty likely survives):**
- No paper puts **declared, sized, addressable omission markers into the serialized document format itself**, as the output of a *deterministic, model-free converter*. All prior art places the mechanism in the **agent harness / retrieval system / memory manager**, which means it requires an agent loop, tool calls, and usually LLM-built indices. TDF's `!E` marker travels inside a plain-text artifact that works in a single-turn, tool-free chat.
- No paper I found reports the **exact token count of the omitted region** in-band so the model can *account* for what it hasn't seen (your Q6 result at `README.md:437-438`). The nearest analogue is your own TOON-derived declared row count.

## Where your approach is most likely already beaten

1. **Sentence-density nav detector (`README.md:368-381`) — this is a rediscovery.** Kohlschütter, Fankhauser & Nejdl, **"Boilerplate detection using shallow text features"**, WSDM 2010, [doi:10.1145/1718487.1718542](https://doi.org/10.1145/1718487.1718542) (verified via OpenAlex W2120101509) established **text density and link density** as the shallow features that separate boilerplate/navigation from main content, competitive with far more complex methods. **jusText** (stopword density + link density) and **trafilatura** (Barbaresi, ACL 2021 System Demos, https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-demo.15/ — title verified) are the mature MIT/Apache implementations. Your sentence-terminator density is a *different and possibly better* signal for non-HTML inputs (it works on PDFs where there is no `<a>` tag), but you must cite this line of work, and you should test **link density as an additional feature** on HTML inputs — it will likely outperform sentence density on the Kubernetes case and cost nothing.
2. **The greedy dictionary vs Re-Pair** — see §2. Your overlap failure (`README.md:459`) is a known-solved problem.
3. **Boilerplate `!R` detection vs suffix automaton** — an O(n) maximal-repeat index is strictly more principled than any threshold-based repeated-line detector, and gives you exact occurrence counts for the MDL test for free.
4. **Skeleton mode vs RAPTOR/PageIndex** — yours is cheaper and deterministic; theirs is measurably better at retrieval (PageIndex: 98.7% FinanceBench). Don't claim retrieval quality without measuring it.

## Replace-a-heuristic-with-an-objective opportunities (ranked)

1. **MDL over a single suffix-automaton index** as the unified objective for `!D` dictionary + `!R` boilerplate + `^` repetition — cite Krimp (DMKD 2011) and SQS (KDD 2012); your existing token-payback inequality *is* the MDL admission rule, so this is mostly reframing plus one data structure.
2. **Token-cost-weighted Re-Pair** replacing greedy seed-and-extend (Larsson & Moffat 1999; bounds from arXiv:1703.06061/1908.06428), gated by (1).
3. **Text-density + link-density + sentence-density as a small feature vector with a threshold fit on labeled regions** instead of a single hand-set `≤0.6 / ≥120 tokens` rule (Kohlschütter 2010).
4. **A grammar that makes malformed tables unrepresentable**, à la OTSL, replacing the "declared row count so truncation is detectable" heuristic.
5. **Benchmark suite**: LongBench + ZeroSCROLLS + GSM8K + LongBench-v2 (structured-data subset) + Table-Meets-LLM, reported as a compression/accuracy curve — replacing the 6-question manual eval in `qa_eval/README_tiering.md`.
